# Child assent guide — draft

**Status: DRAFT. Needs review by the special educator / BCBA advisor
(docs/PLAN.md parallel track) and the partner site's own staff, who know
these specific children, before use — see `README.md` in this folder.**

## Why this isn't a form the child signs

Children aged 4–8 cannot give informed consent in the legal sense — that's
what the parent/guardian consent form is for. But that doesn't mean a young
child's own willingness doesn't matter; it means it has to be checked
differently: **ongoing, behavioural, and non-verbal-inclusive**, not a
one-time signature.

This matters even more for autistic children specifically: communication
style varies widely, "no" may not be spoken, and a child who is
uncomfortable may show it through behaviour rather than words. Anyone
running a session must be watching for that, not just listening for it.

## Before the first session: explaining it simply

Use whatever communication method already works for this specific child
(spoken language, AAC device, picture cards, sign, a combination) — this
guide gives the CONTENT to convey, not a script to read verbatim. Site
staff who already know the child should adapt the delivery.

Simple version of what to convey:

> "We're going to play a counting game on the tablet. Sometimes it will
> look different — different pictures, different ways to answer — that's
> okay, that's part of the game. You can stop and tell [adult] any time you
> want to stop, and that's totally fine."

## Checking willingness — every session, not just once

Before starting:

- Does the child approach the tablet/activity area willingly, or need to be
  physically guided/persuaded? Guided/persuaded → don't start; try again
  another time, or not at all that day.
- If the child uses AAC or another communication support, offer them a
  clear, accessible way to say "not now" or "stop" that they already know
  how to use — don't introduce a new communication method just for this
  study.

During the session, stop immediately (regardless of what the app is doing)
if the child:

- Says or signals "no", "stop", "done", or an equivalent in their own
  communication style.
- Shows clear physical distress signals the adult running the session
  already knows to recognize for this child specifically (this varies
  per child — rely on the site staff's own knowledge, not a generic list).
- Tries to leave, turn away, or push the tablet away.
- Simply seems checked out / non-responsive for a sustained stretch —
  ask, don't assume engagement just because the app is still running.

**A child never has to explain why. Stopping is never followed by
persuasion, bargaining, or "just one more."** This holds even if the app's
own engagement/distress detection hasn't itself flagged anything — the
adult in the room is always the actual safety mechanism; the app's
automatic response (README's distress budget) is a second layer, not a
replacement for one.

## After a session ends early

- No consequence, no note-taking framed as "the child failed to
  participate" — a child choosing to stop is a successful use of their
  right to stop, not a data-collection problem.
- Try again another day if appropriate; don't push to make up "lost" time.

## A note on language

Nothing here, or anywhere else in this study, frames the child's autism
itself as something to manage, reduce, or treat. If a child stims,
that's not a signal to stop for the child's sake unless it's their own way
of communicating "stop" — check with someone who knows the child if
unsure, rather than guessing.
